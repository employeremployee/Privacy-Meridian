## Does New Zealand's Privacy Law Apply to Your Organization? (`nzpa-scope`)

### plainSummary

**Before**

**After**

### professionalLayer

**Before**

> The Privacy Act 2020 applies to agencies, a term covering almost any person or body, public or private, and extends to overseas agencies carrying on business in New Zealand regardless of physical presence or whether they charge for services. Its core is fourteen Information Privacy Principles, numbered 1 to 13 plus 3A. They govern collection, notice of indirect collection, storage, security, access, correction, accuracy, retention, use, disclosure, unique identifiers, and cross-border disclosure. The Privacy Commissioner investigates complaints, issues compliance notices, and can make binding access directions, but the Act carries modest penalties by international standards, with offences capped at NZ$10,000 and no revenue-based fines. New Zealand holds a long-standing EU adequacy decision. A 2025 Privacy Amendment Act adds a new principle, IPP 3A, requiring agencies to tell people when their information is collected from someone other than them, in force since 1 May 2026. A Biometric Processing Privacy Code sets rules for biometric verification, identification and categorisation. It applied to new processing from November 2025, and since August 2026 to processing that began earlier.

## When Organizations May Collect Your Information in New Zealand (`nzpa-ipp1-4`)

### articleRef

**Before**

> IPPs 1-4 and 3A

**After**

> IPPs 1–4 and 3A

### personalImpact

**Before**

> Agencies sometimes gather more information than a task really needs. The Privacy Act says they may collect only what is necessary, and by fair means. You should know why your information is being taken. Collection beyond that is not permitted.

**After**

> Agencies sometimes gather more information than a task really needs. The Privacy Act says they may collect only what is necessary, and by fair means. You should know why your information is being taken.

### professionalLayer

**Before**

> Rather than enumerating legal bases, the Act regulates collection through principles. IPP1 permits collection only for a lawful purpose connected with the agency's functions and only where necessary. IPP2 requires collection directly from the individual, subject to exceptions. IPP3 requires transparency about purpose, recipients, and rights at collection. IPP3A, in force since 1 May 2026, applies when an agency collects information about a person from someone else. The agency must take reasonable steps to tell the person as soon as practicable, unless an exception applies. Exceptions include where the person already knows, the information is publicly available, or notice is not reasonably practicable. IPP3A does not apply to information collected before 1 May 2026. IPP4 prohibits unlawful, unfair, or unreasonably intrusive means, with special regard for children. Consent is not the central gatekeeper; necessity for a lawful purpose is, with use and disclosure limits carried by later principles. This principles-based architecture reads closer to fair information practices than to GDPR.

**After**

> Rather than enumerating legal bases, the Act regulates collection through principles. IPP1 permits collection only for a lawful purpose connected with the agency's functions and only where necessary. IPP2 requires collection directly from the individual, subject to exceptions. IPP3 requires transparency about purpose, recipients, and rights at collection. IPP3A, in force since 1 May 2026, applies when an agency collects information about a person from someone else. The agency must take reasonable steps to tell the person as soon as practicable, unless an exception applies. Exceptions include where the person already knows, the information is publicly available, or notice is not reasonably practicable. IPP3A does not apply to information collected before 1 May 2026. IPP4 prohibits unlawful, unfair, or unreasonably intrusive means, with special regard for children. Necessity, not consent, is the main test. Later principles limit use and disclosure. This principles-based architecture reads closer to fair information practices than to the GDPR.

## What Happens When Your Data Is Compromised in New Zealand (`nzpa-breach`)

### plainSummary

**Before**

> Organizations must notify the Privacy Commissioner and affected people as soon as practicable after a privacy breach that has caused, or is likely to cause, serious harm. Failing to notify the regulator is an offence.

**After**

> Organizations must notify the Privacy Commissioner and affected people as soon as practicable after a privacy breach that has caused, or is likely to cause, serious harm. Failing to notify the regulator is an offense.

### personalImpact

**Before**

> A breach can expose your IRD number, health details, and contact information. A quiet cover-up leaves you defenseless. The Privacy Act requires agencies to notify you and the Commissioner of serious breaches. You are told in time to protect yourself.

**After**

> A breach can expose your IRD number, health details, and contact information. A quiet cover-up leaves you defenseless. The Privacy Act requires agencies to notify you and the Commissioner of serious breaches.

### professionalLayer

**Before**

> Part 6 of the 2020 Act introduced mandatory breach notification for notifiable privacy breaches, defined as breaches it is reasonable to believe have caused or are likely to cause serious harm. Assessment factors include the sensitivity of the information, protective actions taken, and who obtained it. Notification to the Commissioner, via the NotifyUs online tool, and to affected individuals must occur as soon as practicable after awareness, with public notice available where individual notification is not reasonably practicable. Failure to notify the Commissioner without reasonable excuse is an offence with a fine up to NZ$10,000.

**After**

> Part 6 of the 2020 Act introduced mandatory breach notification for notifiable privacy breaches, defined as breaches it is reasonable to believe have caused or are likely to cause serious harm. Assessment factors include the sensitivity of the information, protective actions taken, and who obtained it. Notification to the Commissioner, via the NotifyUs online tool, and to affected individuals must occur as soon as practicable after awareness, with public notice available where individual notification is not reasonably practicable.
