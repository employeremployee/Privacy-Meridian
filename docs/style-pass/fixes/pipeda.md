## Does Canada's Federal Privacy Law Apply to Your Business? (`pipeda-scope`)

### plainSummary

**Before**

> PIPEDA covers businesses that handle personal information in commercial activity across most of Canada. Quebec, Alberta and British Columbia have their own private-sector laws for activity inside those provinces.

**After**

> PIPEDA covers businesses that handle personal information in commercial activity across most of Canada. Quebec, Alberta, and British Columbia have their own private-sector laws for activity inside those provinces.

### professionalLayer

**Before**

> PIPEDA applies to organizations that collect, use or disclose personal information in the course of commercial activities, and to federally regulated employers for their staff data. It has no size threshold. Quebec, Alberta and British Columbia have private-sector laws the federal government has declared substantially similar, and those apply instead for activity inside each province. PIPEDA still applies there to federally regulated businesses and to information crossing provincial or national borders. Ontario, New Brunswick, Newfoundland and Labrador, and Nova Scotia have similar laws for health information. Federal government institutions fall under the separate Privacy Act. Courts apply PIPEDA to foreign organizations with a real and substantial connection to Canada. The Office of the Privacy Commissioner investigates complaints and can enter enforceable compliance agreements, but it cannot issue orders or fines. After a complaint, individuals can go to the Federal Court, which can order changes and award damages. Knowing breaches of key duties, such as breach reporting, are offences with fines up to C$100,000. The EU recognises PIPEDA as adequate for commercial organizations. A bill introduced in June 2026, the Protecting Privacy and Consumer Data Act, would replace PIPEDA's privacy rules, but it is not law. A 2026 amendment adding a data mobility right has passed but is not yet in force. Once it is, it will apply only in sectors the government sets up by regulation.

## When Businesses Need Your Consent in Canada (`pipeda-consent`)

### articleRef

**Before**

> Sections 5-7 and Schedule 1

**After**

> Sections 5–7 and Schedule 1

### plainSummary

**Before**

> Canadian businesses generally need your consent to collect, use or share your personal information. The form of consent depends on how sensitive the information is, with listed exceptions.

**After**

> Canadian businesses generally need your consent to collect, use, or share your personal information. The form of consent depends on how sensitive the information is, with listed exceptions.

### personalImpact

**Before**

> Apps often bury sweeping permissions in long terms. PIPEDA says consent only counts if you could reasonably understand what you agreed to. A business also cannot demand consent beyond what its service needs. Uses a reasonable person would find inappropriate are not allowed at all.

**After**

> Apps often bury sweeping permissions in long terms. PIPEDA says consent only counts if you could reasonably understand what you agreed to. A business also cannot demand consent beyond what its service needs.

### professionalLayer

**Before**

> PIPEDA is built on ten principles in Schedule 1, with consent as the default. Consent is valid only if the people it targets would reasonably understand the nature, purpose and consequences of the collection, use or disclosure. Organizations should generally seek express consent for sensitive information, and implied consent can suit less sensitive information within reasonable expectations. A business cannot make consent a condition of service beyond what the stated, legitimate purpose needs. People can withdraw consent at any time, subject to legal or contractual limits and reasonable notice. Separately, any purpose must be one a reasonable person would consider appropriate, even with consent. Section 7 lists closed exceptions for collection, use and disclosure without consent, such as emergencies, legal requirements, investigations and debt collection. Business transactions and federally regulated employment relationships have their own exceptions. There is no contract or legitimate-interests basis.

**After**

A business cannot make consent a condition of service beyond what the stated, legitimate purpose needs. ## Your Right to Correct Your Information (`pipeda-correction`)

### articleRef

**Before**

> Principles 4.5, 4.6 and 4.9.5

**After**

> Principles 4.5, 4.6, and 4.9.5

### professionalLayer

**Before**

> When a person shows that information is inaccurate or incomplete, the organization must amend it by correcting, deleting or adding information. Where appropriate, it must pass the change to third parties that have the information. If a challenge is not resolved, the organization must record it and, where appropriate, tell those third parties. PIPEDA has no general right to erasure. Information no longer needed for its purposes should be destroyed, erased or made anonymous, and withdrawing consent can lead to deletion. Quebec's law goes further: it gives a right to deletion where keeping the data is not authorized by law, and a right to have certain information de-indexed.

**After**

## What Happens When Your Data Is Breached (`pipeda-breach`)

### articleRef

**Before**

> Division 1.1 (Sections 10.1-10.3)

**After**

> Division 1.1, Sections 10.1–10.3

### personalImpact

**Before**

> A breach can expose your bank details, health data or account passwords. Silence gives fraudsters a head start. PIPEDA requires the business to warn you when the risk of harm is real. You learn in time to protect yourself.

**After**

> A breach can expose your bank details, health data, or account passwords. Silence gives fraudsters a head start. PIPEDA requires the business to warn you when the risk of harm is real.

### professionalLayer

**Before**

> Mandatory breach notification began in November 2018. An organization must report a breach of security safeguards to the Privacy Commissioner, and notify affected individuals, where it is reasonable to believe the breach creates a real risk of significant harm. Significant harm includes humiliation, reputational damage, financial loss, identity theft and damage to credit. The test weighs how sensitive the information is and how likely it is to be misused. Both notices are due as soon as feasible after the organization determines the breach occurred, with no fixed hour count. Notices to individuals must be direct unless that would cause further harm or undue hardship. Organizations must also tell other bodies that can reduce the harm. They must keep a record of every breach, notifiable or not, for 24 months and show it to the regulator on request. Knowingly failing to report, notify or keep records is an offence with fines up to C$100,000.

**After**

## Sending Your Information outside Canada (`pipeda-transfers`)

### surfaceLabel

**Before**

> Sending Your Information Outside Canada

**After**

> Sending Your Information outside Canada

### professionalLayer

**Before**

> PIPEDA has no transfer-specific rules, adequacy list, standard clauses or certification scheme. Under the accountability principle, an organization stays responsible for information it transfers to a third party for processing. It must use contractual or other means to provide a comparable level of protection. The regulator treats a transfer for processing as a use, not a disclosure, so it needs no separate consent. It does need openness: people should be told their information may be processed abroad and reached by foreign authorities. The regulator considered requiring consent for transfers in 2019 but kept its existing guidance. Quebec is stricter: before communicating personal information outside Quebec, an organization must assess the privacy impact and may proceed only if protection would be adequate, under a written agreement.

**After**
