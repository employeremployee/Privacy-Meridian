## Does Switzerland's Privacy Law Apply to Your Organization? (`fadp-scope`)

### personalImpact

**Before**

> Switzerland has long treated privacy as part of personal dignity, not just paperwork. Yet your data still flows to banks, insurers, and online services daily. The revised FADP covers private organizations and federal bodies whose handling of data affects people in Switzerland. Cantonal and communal bodies follow cantonal law instead. It sets modern rules built on that strong privacy tradition.

**After**

> Switzerland has long treated privacy as part of personal dignity, not just paperwork. Yet your data still flows to banks, insurers, and online services daily. The revised FADP covers private organizations and federal bodies whose handling of data affects people in Switzerland. Cantonal and communal bodies follow cantonal law instead.

### professionalLayer

**Before**

> The revised FADP modernized Swiss data protection to maintain alignment with European standards while keeping a distinctly Swiss structure. It applies to circumstances that have an effect in Switzerland, even if initiated abroad, giving it extraterritorial reach. Foreign controllers meeting certain thresholds must designate a Swiss representative. Like the GDPR, and unlike the old Swiss law, it protects only natural persons. Unlike the GDPR, it has no general prohibition model. It enforces mainly through criminal fines of up to CHF 250,000 on responsible individuals, and only for intentional breaches. Most offences are prosecuted only if someone files a complaint. A business itself can be fined up to CHF 50,000 when finding the responsible person would take disproportionate effort. Cantonal authorities prosecute and impose these fines. The Federal Data Protection and Information Commissioner supervises compliance and can investigate and issue binding orders.

## When Swiss Law Allows Your Data to Be Used (`fadp-lawfulness`)

### articleRef

**Before**

> Articles 30-31

**After**

> Articles 30–31

### plainSummary

**Before**

> Swiss law works differently from GDPR. Private organizations may generally process personal data without a specific legal basis, as long as they follow the processing principles and do not unlawfully harm the person's privacy.

**After**

> Swiss law works differently from the GDPR. Private organizations may generally process personal data without a specific legal basis, as long as they follow the processing principles and do not unlawfully harm the person's privacy.

### personalImpact

**Before**

> A company cannot treat your data as freely usable simply because it holds it. Under Swiss law, processing must respect your personality and stay within a justifiable purpose. Consent, a contract, or an overriding interest can justify it. Handling that oversteps those limits is unlawful.

**After**

> A company cannot treat your data as freely usable simply because it holds it. Under Swiss law, processing must respect your personality and stay within a justifiable purpose. Consent, a contract, or an overriding interest can justify it.

### professionalLayer

**Before**

> The FADP does not adopt GDPR's prohibition-with-legal-bases model. Private-sector processing is permitted by default if it complies with the processing principles: lawfulness, good faith, proportionality, purpose limitation and recognizability, storage limitation, accuracy, and security. Processing that breaches the principles, goes against the data subject's express wish, or discloses sensitive data to third parties is a personality violation. It then needs justification by consent, an overriding private or public interest, or law. Where consent is relied on, it must be informed and voluntary. It must be explicit for sensitive data or high-risk profiling. Federal bodies, by contrast, need a statutory basis. This inversion of the GDPR model is the FADP's most distinctive feature.

**After**

> The FADP does not adopt the GDPR's prohibition-with-legal-bases model. Private-sector processing is permitted by default if it complies with the processing principles: lawfulness, good faith, proportionality, purpose limitation and recognizability, storage limitation, accuracy, and security. Processing that breaches the principles, goes against the data subject's express wish, or discloses sensitive data to third parties is a personality violation. It then needs justification by consent, an overriding private or public interest, or law. Where consent is relied on, it must be informed and voluntary. It must be explicit for sensitive data or high-risk profiling. Federal bodies, by contrast, need a statutory basis. This inverts the GDPR model.

## What Happens When Your Data Is Compromised in Switzerland (`fadp-breach`)

### professionalLayer

**Before**

> Article 24 requires controllers to notify the FDPIC as soon as possible of breaches likely to lead to a high risk to the data subject's personality or fundamental rights. This is a higher threshold and a softer deadline than the GDPR, which requires notice within 72 hours where feasible unless a breach is unlikely to pose a risk. Data subjects must be informed where necessary for their protection or where the FDPIC requests it. The controller may limit, delay or skip that notice in some cases, such as when a public announcement does the same job. Processors must report breaches to controllers as soon as possible. Notifications to the FDPIC are made through its online portal, and the notification itself cannot be used against the notifier in criminal proceedings without consent. Since April 2025, the FDPIC may forward a report to the Federal Office for Cybersecurity, with the controller’s consent.

**After**

## Moving Personal Data Out of Switzerland (`fadp-transfers`)

### articleRef

**Before**

> Articles 16-18

**After**

> Articles 16–18

### personalImpact

**Before**

> Your data often leaves Switzerland for servers elsewhere. Abroad, it may fall under weaker protection. Swiss law allows the transfer only where adequate safeguards remain in place. Your protections stay with your data across the border.

**After**

> Your data often leaves Switzerland for servers elsewhere. Abroad, it may fall under weaker protection. Swiss law allows the transfer only where adequate safeguards remain in place.

### professionalLayer

**Before**

> Cross-border disclosure is permitted where the Federal Council has listed the destination state as providing adequate protection, a list published in an annex to the ordinance. Absent adequacy, transfers require safeguards including standard data protection clauses accepted by the FDPIC, where the EU SCCs adapted for Switzerland are common practice, binding corporate rules approved by the FDPIC, codes of conduct or certification, or specific derogations such as explicit consent or contract performance. Since September 2024, the US counts as adequate for organizations certified under the Swiss-US Data Privacy Framework. The overall architecture parallels GDPR Chapter V with Swiss-specific paperwork.

**After**

> Cross-border disclosure is permitted where the Federal Council has listed the destination state as providing adequate protection, a list published in an annex to the ordinance.
